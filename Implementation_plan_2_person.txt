Person 1 — Investigation & Intelligence

Person 1 is essentially building the "brain" of TRACE — the part that takes a KPI change and investigates why it happened.

1. Synthetic Business Dataset
1. Create realistic business data

You need simulated data representing a company. For example:

Revenue
Orders
Average Order Value
Product/category
Region
Inventory
Delivery performance
Customer complaints
Customer reviews
Competitor pricing

You don't need a huge dataset. For the prototype, something like 6–12 months × several regions × several products is sufficient.

2. Deliberately inject a business problem

Create a known scenario such as:

North India

Revenue ↓ 8.2%
AOV ↓ 6.1%
Premium sales ↓ 19%
Delivery SLA breaches ↑ 27%
Complaints about delivery ↑ 34%

The important thing is that the dataset should contain hidden relationships that TRACE has to discover.

For example, don't explicitly label:

"Delivery problems caused revenue decline."

Instead, provide the underlying data and let the system infer that delivery reliability is a strong hypothesis.

3. Create both structured and unstructured data

You need two fundamentally different types:

Structured:
sales.csv
orders.csv
inventory.csv
delivery.csv
pricing.csv

Unstructured:
customer reviews
support tickets
emails
internal reports

This is essential because your problem statement specifically requires the engine to use both structured and unstructured data.

2. KPI / Signal Engine

This is the part that answers:

"Did something actually change, or are we just seeing normal business noise?"

1. Calculate KPI movement

Given historical data, calculate things like:

Revenue:
Previous month = ₹10M
Current month  = ₹9.18M

Change = -8.2%

Also calculate related KPIs:

Orders
AOV
Conversion
Refunds
Inventory
Delivery SLA

This gives TRACE the raw signal.

2. Determine whether the change is meaningful

Don't simply say:

Revenue decreased 8.2%, therefore something is wrong.

Compare it against historical behavior.

For example:

Historical average = ₹10M
Normal range       = ₹9.7M–₹10.3M
Current revenue    = ₹9.18M

Now TRACE can say:

This is outside the normal historical range.

You can implement this initially with rolling averages, standard deviation/z-score, seasonality and business thresholds.

3. Produce a machine-readable signal

The engine should ultimately produce something like:

{
  "kpi": "Revenue",
  "change_pct": -8.2,
  "is_anomaly": true,
  "severity": "high"
}

This becomes the input for everything that follows.

3. RAG Pipeline

This is how TRACE investigates the unstructured information.

1. Convert business documents into searchable knowledge

Take:

Customer reviews
Support tickets
Emails
Reports

and process them:

Documents
↓
Clean text
↓
Chunks
↓
Embeddings
↓
Vector database

For a prototype, FAISS or Chroma + an embedding model is enough.

2. Retrieve evidence relevant to the KPI problem

Suppose the signal is:

Revenue ↓ 8.2%

TRACE should generate investigation questions such as:

Why did premium sales decline?
Were there delivery problems?
Did customers complain?
Did competitors change prices?
Was inventory unavailable?

Then retrieve relevant documents.

For example:

"My premium order arrived four days late."

That becomes evidence supporting the delivery reliability hypothesis.

3. Return evidence with source information

Don't just give the LLM raw text.

Return:

{
  "text": "Premium order arrived four days late...",
  "source": "support_ticket_1042",
  "date": "2026-08-12",
  "region": "North India",
  "topic": "delivery"
}

This allows the UI to say:

Evidence: 12 support tickets mentioning delayed premium deliveries.

It also makes your AI explanation more trustworthy.

4. Hypothesis Engine

This is where TRACE moves from:

"Here are some correlations."

to:

"Here are the most plausible explanations."

1. Generate candidate explanations

For:

Revenue ↓ 8.2%

TRACE might generate:

H1 — Delivery reliability
H2 — Competitor pricing
H3 — Conversion decline
H4 — Inventory shortage
H5 — Seasonality

You can initially define a fixed set of business hypotheses rather than making everything completely autonomous.

2. Test each hypothesis against evidence

For example:

Delivery reliability

SLA breaches       +27% ✓
Delivery complaints +34% ✓
Premium sales       -19% ✓

Therefore, this hypothesis gets strong support.

But perhaps:

Customer satisfaction remained stable ✗

which becomes contradictory evidence.

The engine should therefore evaluate evidence FOR and AGAINST each hypothesis.

3. Rank the hypotheses

Finally produce:

1. Delivery reliability      78%
2. Competitor pricing        61%
3. Conversion decline        44%
4. Inventory shortage        32%
5. Seasonality               21%

Important:

These percentages should represent support/confidence, not claim that you have mathematically proven causality.

Your language should be:

"Delivery reliability is the strongest supported hypothesis."

not:

"Delivery problems caused the revenue decline."

5. Evidence Scoring

This is the layer that determines:

"How strong is the evidence behind this explanation?"

1. Score different types of evidence

For example:

Structured KPI evidence
Temporal evidence
Segment-level evidence
RAG/document evidence
External evidence

Suppose:

SLA breaches ↑ 27%

That's strong structured evidence.

And:

12 customers complained about delayed premium deliveries.

That's unstructured evidence.

Both support the same hypothesis.

2. Consider evidence quality

Not every piece of evidence should have equal weight.

For example:

Direct operational metric      → Strong
Multiple independent tickets   → Strong
Single customer review         → Weak
Old report from 2 years ago    → Weak
Unverified external information → Weak

You can therefore score things such as:

Relevance
Recency
Reliability
Independence
Consistency
3. Aggregate evidence into hypothesis support

For example:

Delivery reliability

Structured evidence     +0.30
Customer evidence       +0.25
Temporal alignment      +0.15
Segment consistency     +0.10
Contradictory evidence  -0.05

Final support = 0.75

This gives you the basis for the hypothesis confidence.

6. Uncertainty / Contradiction Detection

This is one of the most important parts of the whole project.

Your system must know when it doesn't know.

1. Identify missing or weak data

For example:

Competitor delivery data = unavailable
Customer history = incomplete
Inventory records = only 60% complete

TRACE should explicitly say:

Data completeness: 62%

instead of pretending the analysis is perfect.

2. Find evidence that disagrees

Example:

Evidence FOR:
SLA breaches ↑27%

Evidence AGAINST:
Customer satisfaction unchanged

TRACE should detect that conflict.

Then:

Confidence:
78% → 69%

This is much better than ignoring contradictory evidence.

3. Know when not to give a definitive answer

If the evidence is genuinely ambiguous:

Delivery reliability       46%
Competitor pricing         43%
Conversion decline         39%

TRACE should say:

No single root cause can be established from the available evidence.

Then recommend:

Collect competitor pricing data before taking a major operational action.

That directly addresses one of the hardest questions in the problem statement.

Person 2 — Decision Experience

Person 2 is building the "decision layer" — how the investigation becomes something a business leader can understand, challenge and act upon.

1. Dashboard

The dashboard answers:

"What is happening in my business?"

1. Show important KPIs and anomalies

For example:

Revenue
₹9.18M
↓ 8.2%
HIGH IMPACT

Also:

Orders       ↓ 2%
AOV          ↓ 6.1%
Premium      ↓ 19%

Don't make this a generic BI dashboard. The dashboard should emphasize changes that require investigation.

2. Provide historical context

Show:

Revenue trend

₹10M ──────────────
             \
              \
               ₹9.18M

and indicate the normal historical range.

The user should immediately understand:

"This isn't just a small fluctuation."

3. Give the user an entry point into investigation

The important button is:

[ Investigate ]

Clicking it should take the user from:

What happened?

to:

Why did it happen?

2. KPI Storytelling

This is where you convert analytics into natural language business explanation.

1. Explain what changed

Example:

Revenue decreased 8.2% in North India compared with the previous month and is significantly below its historical baseline.

This is generated from actual analytical results.

2. Explain the major contributors

Instead of just saying:

Revenue declined.

Say:

The decline was primarily associated with a 6.1% decrease in AOV and a 19% decrease in premium-category sales, while order volume declined only 2%.

This connects the KPI to its drivers.

3. Present the story with evidence and uncertainty

The final story might say:

Delivery reliability is the strongest supported explanation, with SLA breaches increasing 27% and delivery-related complaints increasing 34%. However, competitor delivery data is unavailable, so this should be treated as a supported hypothesis rather than confirmed causation.

This is much more credible than generic LLM-generated commentary.

3. Driver Decomposition Visualization

This visually answers:

"What contributed to the KPI movement?"

1. Break the KPI into components

For revenue:

Revenue
  │
  ├── Orders
  ├── AOV
  ├── Product mix
  ├── Pricing
  └── Refunds

You can use a waterfall chart here.

2. Show magnitude of contribution

For example:

Revenue change = -8.2%

AOV              -6.1%
Premium sales    -2.4%
Orders           -2.0%
Pricing           +0.6%
Refunds           -0.3%

The user should immediately see which drivers matter.

3. Allow drill-down

Click:

Premium sales ↓19%

and go deeper:

Premium sales ↓19%
        ↓
North India
        ↓
Delivery SLA ↑27%
        ↓
Customer complaints ↑34%

This connects the visualization to the investigation engine.

4. Evidence Graph

This is the visual representation of:

"Why does TRACE believe this hypothesis?"

1. Connect KPI → drivers → evidence → hypothesis

Example:

Revenue ↓8.2%
       ↓
Premium Sales ↓19%
       ↓
Delivery SLA ↑27%
       ↓
Complaints ↑34%
       ↓
Delivery Reliability

This allows the user to visually follow the reasoning chain.

2. Make evidence nodes clickable

For example, clicking:

SLA breaches +27%

opens:

Source: Operations DB
Period: July–August
Region: North India
Value: +27%

Clicking a customer complaint can show its original text.

3. Show supporting vs contradictory relationships

For example:

GREEN/supporting
───────────────→

RED/contradictory
- - - - - - - - →

This makes uncertainty visually understandable instead of hiding it in a confidence percentage.

5. Challenge Conclusion

This should be one of the signature features of TRACE.

1. Allow the user to challenge the AI

Display:

Strongest hypothesis:
Delivery reliability

Confidence: 78%

[ Challenge Conclusion ]

The user is effectively asking:

"Prove that you're not jumping to the obvious conclusion."

2. Search for alternative explanations

When clicked, TRACE should investigate:

Competitor pricing
Inventory shortage
Seasonality
Conversion

and search for evidence against the original hypothesis.

For example:

Competitor prices decreased 12% in the same period.

That could weaken the original conclusion.

3. Recalculate the conclusion

Before:

Delivery reliability   78%
Competitor pricing     61%

After finding contradictory evidence:

Delivery reliability   68%
Competitor pricing     67%

Then TRACE can say:

Conclusion is ambiguous. No single cause has sufficient evidence.

This is a major differentiator.

6. Recommendation Engine + Decision Memory

This is where TRACE answers the final business question:

"So what should I do?"

1. Convert hypotheses into concrete actions

Don't say:

"Improve delivery."

Instead:

Investigate premium-category SLA breaches in North India and identify the three highest-delay distribution centers.

Recommendations should be:

Specific
Actionable
Prioritized
2. Rank actions by business value and confidence

For example:

Action	Impact	Urgency	Confidence
Investigate premium SLA breaches	High	High	81%
Contact affected customers	Medium	High	74%
Review competitor pricing	Medium	Medium	67%
Increase inventory	Low	Low	32%

The system should not recommend an expensive intervention when the evidence is weak.

3. Store the decision and learn from the outcome

When the user chooses:

Take Action

store:

KPI:
Revenue ↓8.2%

Hypothesis:
Delivery reliability

Confidence:
81%

Action:
Investigate premium SLA breaches

Outcome:
Revenue recovered +5.7%

Later, TRACE can use this historical decision as decision memory.

This is what makes the system move from:

AI analysis

toward:

organizational intelligence.

1. Do you need a chatbot?
Short answer: I strongly recommend adding one, but it should not be the core of TRACE.

The problem statement does not explicitly require a chatbot.

It says:

"Proactive alerts, conversational analysis, augmented dashboards or decision workspaces."

That means conversational analysis is one possible interface.

For your hackathon, however, a chatbot is valuable because it demonstrates:

"TRACE isn't just showing an explanation — a business user can interrogate the explanation."

For example:

User:
Why did revenue fall in North India?

TRACE:
Revenue declined 8.2% in North India.

Top supported drivers:
1. Premium sales ↓19%
2. Delivery SLA breaches ↑27%
3. Orders ↓2%

Confidence: 78%

Evidence:
...

Then:

User:
Could competitor pricing explain this instead?

TRACE:
Competitor pricing is a plausible alternative hypothesis,
but current evidence is insufficient.

Confidence: 61%

Missing evidence:
Competitor price data for Aug 2026.

Then:

User:
What should we do?

TRACE:
Prioritize investigation of premium delivery SLA breaches.

Expected impact: High
Confidence: 81%
Owner: Regional Operations

That is much more compelling than a generic ChatGPT-style chatbot.
